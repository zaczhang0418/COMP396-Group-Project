# Section 3.3.3 Draft Text

The overspending problem in Team01 V2 is more precisely described as persistent capital over-commitment rather than outright bankruptcy. Figures 3.3.3a and 3.3.3b, together with Table 3.3.3a, show that V2 operated much closer to the full-capital boundary than V3 throughout Part 3. V2 recorded an average gross exposure of 46.83%, while its 95th-percentile gross exposure rose to 99.39%, and the portfolio spent 62 trading days above 95% gross exposure. V3, by contrast, maintained a substantially lighter capital profile, with average gross exposure of 16.72%, a 95th-percentile level of 54.31%, and no days above 95% gross exposure. This contrast is important because it indicates that the V2 allocation logic left little headroom for adverse gaps, slippage, or overlapping rebalance demands, whereas V3 preserved a materially larger operating buffer.

The operational consequences of this over-commitment are shown in Figures 3.3.3b and 3.3.3c. V2 generated 170 multi-order days and 148 days on which the same series was targeted more than once within the same session, compared with only 11 and 0 respectively in V3. Figure 3.3.3c provides a concrete illustration from an early Part 3 window in which repeated same-series trend-following rebalancing occurred on consecutive days while overall capital usage was already elevated. This pattern suggests that the strategy was not simply taking large positions, but was also repeatedly reallocating capital under already tight budget conditions. Even though the framework prevented literal negative-cash execution, the V2 portfolio still displayed clear signs of order-churn pressure and weak capital discipline.

The underlying cause was structural. In V2, the TF, MR, and GARCH legs each adjusted exposure through independent `order_target_percent` calls under static leg weights, yet there was no explicit portfolio-level gross cap to coordinate the total capital commitment across legs. The framework-level overspend guard only blocked orders when forecast next-open cash would become negative, meaning that it acted as a reactive safety check rather than as a proactive budgeting mechanism. V3 addressed this weakness more directly by computing desired allocations jointly, scaling them under `gross_cap=1.00`, and applying a rebalance tolerance to reduce unnecessary trading. The main conclusion for Section 3.3.3 is therefore that V2 suffered from overspending pressure because its capital allocation process remained too close to the full-budget boundary and too prone to repeated rebalancing, whereas V3 improved robustness by explicitly constraining both gross exposure and order churn.
